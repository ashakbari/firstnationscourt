## A. What First Nations Court is

- A1. There are ten specialized sentencing courts in BC for Indigenous people charged with criminal offences. Quote: "There are ten specialized sentencing courts in BC for Indigenous people charged with criminal offences." (PC main page)
- A2. The Provincial Court's umbrella term is "Indigenous Sentencing Courts". Individual courts keep their own names; some are called First Nations Court, some Indigenous Court, one First Nations Sentencing Court. No official source explains the naming difference; it simply varies by location. (PC main page)
- A3. These courts do not conduct trials. Quote: "These courts do not conduct trials." (PC main page)
- A4. Legal Aid BC describes them: "First Nations/Indigenous Courts are criminal sentencing courts that use restorative justice and traditional ways to reach balance and healing." They are "often called Gladue courts". (https://aboriginal.legalaid.bc.ca/courts-criminal-cases/first-nations-court)
- A5. The healing plan is central. Quote: "The healing plan is at the heart of BC's Indigenous Courts. It balances the legal requirements of the traditional court system with Indigenous ways and laws." (PC main page)
- A6. A 2024 report, Reconciliation in Action, examined the stories of 20 participants at the New Westminster court. Quote: "It concluded that the involvement of elders, healing plans, Indigenous services and wrap-around client support had proven to be important factors in reducing participants' risk of reoffending." (https://provincialcourt.bc.ca/news-notices-policies-and-practice-directions/enews/22-12-2025)
- A7. Elders' role: "Elders act as advisors to the judge and provide support to the offender, but they also hold the offender accountable." (PC main page)
- A8. Development partners include Indigenous communities, Elders, Legal Aid BC, the BC government, Crown and defence lawyers, Community Corrections, police, and the Native Courtworker and Counselling Association of BC. (PC main page)

## C. Who can use it

- C1. The Provincial Court's eligibility wording: "They are courts for people who identify as Indigenous, indicate an intention to plead guilty to a criminal offence and depending on the specific court, have Crown counsel's agreement to participate." (PC main page)
- C2. "Indigenous" is by self-identification and includes status and non-status First Nations, Métis, and Inuit. Quote: "The terms 'Aboriginal' and 'Indigenous' mean the same thing and include status and non-status, First Nations, Métis, and Inuit." (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/resources/indigenous-peoples/what-are-first-nations-indigenous-courts)
- C3. It applies whether you live on or off reserve. (https://wiki.clicklaw.bc.ca/index.php/Justice_Systems_(Legal_Information_for_Indigenous_People))
- C4. Offence scope: in each community an Advisory Committee works with Elders to decide what offences the court will deal with. Quote: "Indigenous courts usually deal with less serious offences." No official source publishes a list of excluded offences. (https://provincialcourt.bc.ca/news-notices-policies-and-practice-directions/enews/22-12-2025)
- C5. Kamloops court participation criteria from a Justice Canada evaluation: self-identify as Indigenous, have pleaded guilty, be charged with an offence triable in provincial criminal court, and "be willing to comply with the Court's sentence and the healing plan." (https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/rp-pr/cp-pm/eval/rep-rap/2016/lap-paj/p11.html)

## D. The responsibility requirement

Wording varies across official sources. The site reflects the variation honestly and tells readers a lawyer will explain what applies to them.

- D1. Provincial Court: participants "indicate an intention to plead guilty to a criminal offence". (PC main page)
- D2. Provincial Court eNews: "The Indigenous person must plead guilty, and the prosecutor must agree to move their case to the Indigenous court." (https://provincialcourt.bc.ca/news-notices-policies-and-practice-directions/enews/22-12-2025)
- D3. Legal Aid BC: these are courts "for Indigenous people who plead guilty or are found guilty" and "You must accept that you're responsible for your actions." (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/resources/indigenous-peoples/what-are-first-nations-indigenous-courts)
- D4. Legal Aid BC warning: "Don't plead guilty without getting legal advice first." (https://aboriginal.legalaid.bc.ca/courts-criminal-cases/first-nations-court)
- D5. Mainly a sentencing and review court, but Legal Aid BC says a bail or sentencing hearing might be held there, and Justice Canada says referral is possible "for most bail and sentencing hearings". (https://aboriginal.legalaid.bc.ca/courts-criminal-cases/first-nations-court and https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/rp-pr/jr/gladue/p4.html)
- D6. In some cases clients complete healing plans while on bail and Crown counsel do not proceed with their charges. (PC main page)

## E. Getting a case there

- E1. Referral can come from a judge, defence lawyer, or Crown counsel, or the person can ask. Quote: "You might be brought into a First Nations/Indigenous Court by referral from a judge, defence lawyer, or Crown lawyer. Or you can request to go to First Nations/Indigenous Court." (https://aboriginal.legalaid.bc.ca/courts-criminal-cases/first-nations-court)
- E2. The practical route: "You or your lawyer can call First Nations Court duty counsel to see if you can go to First Nations/Indigenous Court. They can tell you how to get your case transferred there." Numbers: 604-601-6074 (Greater Vancouver), 1-877-601-6066 (elsewhere in BC). (https://aboriginal.legalaid.bc.ca/courts-criminal-cases/first-nations-court)
- E3. Duty counsel can explain the court's requirements so the person can decide whether to ask for a transfer. (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/resources/indigenous-peoples/what-are-first-nations-indigenous-courts)
- E4. Crown agreement: the December 2025 eNews states it without qualification ("the prosecutor must agree to move their case to the Indigenous court"), while the main page qualifies it ("depending on the specific court"). The site says Crown counsel usually has to agree. (D2; C1)
- E5. A dated Clicklaw page (last reviewed March 2017, marked no longer updated) describes telling the judge at your next appearance and a "Request for Waiver" form with Crown approval when the offence happened outside a First Nations Court location. Use only with the date flagged; prefer the duty counsel route in E2. (https://wiki.clicklaw.bc.ca/index.php?title=I_Want_My_Criminal_Case_to_Be_Dealt_with_in_First_Nations_Court)
- E6. No official source describes extra procedural steps between the request and the first First Nations Court appearance. Do not invent any. Courts sit on scheduled dates (B11, B12), so there is usually a wait for the next sitting.

## F. The hearing

- F1. Circle format: "Offenders (referred to as clients) usually sit in a circle with the judge, Elders, lawyers, and others. The circle may include victims, family members, and support people." (PC main page)
- F2. Core team per Legal Aid BC: "Elders, your lawyer (or duty counsel if you don't have a lawyer), the judge, and Crown counsel (government lawyer)". Others who may take part: Indigenous community members, social workers, Native courtworkers, probation officers, victim services workers. (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/resources/indigenous-peoples/what-are-first-nations-indigenous-courts)
- F3. Supporters: "You can bring family, friends, community members, Elders, and other support people to First Nations/Indigenous Court. They'll have a chance to speak in court, if they want." (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/resources/indigenous-peoples/what-are-first-nations-indigenous-courts)
- F4. Ceremony: "Court may begin and end with a prayer. It may include a ceremony used by Indigenous groups to cleanse the spirit and bring forth good energy, such as smudging." (PC main page)
- F5. What the judge weighs: "the harm you caused to the victim(s), your background, your strengths and resilience, your needs now, and how a healing plan can be made for you and your community." (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/resources/indigenous-peoples/what-are-first-nations-indigenous-courts)
- F6. Attendance is in person by default. (https://aboriginal.legalaid.bc.ca/courts-criminal-cases/first-nations-court, citing the Provincial Court's NP 28)
- F7. Eagle feathers and the counterclockwise order of introductions appear in no official source for BC's courts. They come from AUTHOR-NOTES.md (firsthand at New Westminster). State them tied to New Westminster with "may" or "often" wording, never as how every court runs, and without "this site's author" phrasing.

## G. Healing plans

- G1. The circle helps create the plan: "Together, they create a healing plan that may be part of the judge's sentence." (PC main page)
- G2. Contents may include sweats, a healing circle, community service, or counselling. (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/resources/indigenous-peoples/what-are-first-nations-indigenous-courts)
- G3. A probation order example from the court: "a probation order might include attending sweat lodges, undergoing treatment for substance use disorder and steps to reconnect with an Indigenous community." (PC main page)
- G4. Elders who have completed an orientation program work with the person, the lawyers, and Native courtworkers to devise the plan, and "The judge often incorporates the healing plan in the sentence they impose." (https://provincialcourt.bc.ca/news-notices-policies-and-practice-directions/enews/22-12-2025)
- G5. Legal vehicles verified: bail conditions, probation orders, conditional sentence orders. Quote: clients are supervised "while on bail, probation, or a conditional sentence order". "Suspended sentence" is not verified by any listed source; do not use the term. (https://provincialcourt.bc.ca/news-notices-policies-and-practice-directions/enews/clients-stories-reveal-indigenous-courts-impact-0)
- G6. Purpose framing from Legal Aid BC: "A healing plan may help you, your community, and the victim(s) of your crime move on. You'll work on the issues that got you into trouble with the law in the first place." (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/resources/indigenous-peoples/what-are-first-nations-indigenous-courts)

## H. Review hearings

- H1. The person returns to report progress "usually every few months" and "they receive feedback from Elders and the judge". (PC main page)
- H2. Reviews are required: "As part of your plan, regular reviews after your sentencing are required. This allows the Elders, judge, and lawyers to check on your progress and support you." (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/resources/indigenous-peoples/what-are-first-nations-indigenous-courts)
- H3. "In most First Nations/Indigenous Courts, you return to the court several times, so the judge and Elders can see your progress with your healing plan." (https://aboriginal.legalaid.bc.ca/courts-criminal-cases/first-nations-court)
- H4. Regular reviews increase supervision and accountability while on bail, probation, or a conditional sentence order. (https://provincialcourt.bc.ca/news-notices-policies-and-practice-directions/enews/clients-stories-reveal-indigenous-courts-impact-0)
- H5. Completion: "A client who successfully completes a healing plan graduates from the Indigenous Court and may have a blanket ceremony symbolizing a new beginning." The court says the blanket, wrapped by an Elder, symbolizes "the client's acceptance of responsibility, support, protection, growth and a welcoming back to the community." (PC main page)

## I. When someone slips up

No official source describes a breach procedure specific to these courts. The site may state only the following verifiable chain, and must not claim as fact that a case "goes back to regular court".

- I1. Healing plan conditions live in bail, probation, or conditional sentence orders (G5).
- I2. Under general BC law, breaching such an order is itself a criminal charge. Quote: "Judges view a breach of a court order as a serious matter." Maximum penalties: on summary conviction, two years less a day in jail or up to a $5,000 fine or both; by indictment, up to two years for breach of bail or four years for breach of probation. (https://info.legalaid.bc.ca/info/breach-court-order)
- I3. Participation requires willingness to comply with the sentence and the healing plan (C5).
- I4. Expectation-setting from a dated source (2017, flagged): "Do not assume that you will get a lighter sentence in First Nations Court. The Elders and the Judge want to help you in a healing journey but will hold you accountable for what you did." Note: the site never uses the phrase "healing journey"; paraphrase within the quote's meaning. (https://wiki.clicklaw.bc.ca/index.php?title=I_Want_My_Criminal_Case_to_Be_Dealt_with_in_First_Nations_Court)
- I5. Elders and the judge hold the person accountable (A7).

## J. Gladue

- J1. Criminal Code s. 718.2(e), official text: sentencing courts must consider "all available sanctions, other than imprisonment, that are reasonable in the circumstances and consistent with the harm done to victims or to the community ... with particular attention to the circumstances of Aboriginal offenders." (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/section-718.2.html)
- J2. R v Gladue, [1999] 1 SCR 688: the Supreme Court of Canada held that s. 718.2(e) is remedial and aimed at over-incarceration of Indigenous people; judges must consider unique systemic and background factors and appropriate sentencing procedures and sanctions; it applies whether the person lives on or off reserve. (https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/rp-pr/jr/gladue/p2.html; case at https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/1999/1999canlii679/1999canlii679.html)
- J3. R v Ipeelee, 2012 SCC 13: reaffirmed Gladue; applying Gladue factors is mandatory, not optional, regardless of the seriousness of the offence, and failing to apply them makes a sentence unfit. (https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/rp-pr/jr/gladue/p2.html; case at https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2012/2012scc13/2012scc13.html)
- J4. Gladue applies in every court: "If you identify as Indigenous and are charged with a crime, the judge must apply Gladue principles." Courts have applied Gladue principles at bail, sentencing, appeals, parole hearings, and more. (https://aboriginal.legalaid.bc.ca/courts-criminal-cases/gladue-rights)
- J5. Gladue rights belong to First Nations, Métis, and Inuit people who self-identify, on or off reserve, in Indigenous or non-Indigenous communities. (https://bcfnjc.com/gladue-services/)
- J6. Saying no to First Nations Court does not remove Gladue rights, and a person who does not want a Gladue report is still covered: "If you do not want a Gladue Report, you are still covered by section 718.2(e) of the Criminal Code." (https://wiki.clicklaw.bc.ca/index.php/Justice_Systems_(Legal_Information_for_Indigenous_People); J4)
- J7. A Gladue report is a detailed report for the court with "comprehensive information about the individual before the court, their community, and a plan that looks at realistic and viable options (other than jail) for the judge to consider." The site paraphrases without the word "comprehensive". (https://legalaid.bc.ca/lawyers/gladueReportProgram)
- J8. Since April 1, 2021, the Gladue reports program in BC is administered by the BC First Nations Justice Council. (https://legalaid.bc.ca/lawyers/gladueReportProgram and https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2021AG0023-000582)
- J9. Requesting a report: through your lawyer, who refers via BCFNJC's system; "If you do not have a lawyer, BC Prosecution Service (BCPS) can also help submit a Gladue request on your behalf." BCFNJC Gladue services: 1-877-602-4858 or 1-778-940-3519. (https://bcfnjc.com/gladue-services/)
- J10. BCFNJC writes Gladue reports for sentencing hearings, reviews in First Nations/Indigenous court, dangerous offender hearings, long term offender hearings, and not criminally responsible hearings, but not for bail hearings, even though Gladue principles still apply at bail. (https://bcfnjc.com/gladue-services/; J4)
- J11. Reports can take up to 8 weeks. Requesting one is the person's right, but reports are not required. (https://wiki.clicklaw.bc.ca/index.php/Justice_Systems_(Legal_Information_for_Indigenous_People))
